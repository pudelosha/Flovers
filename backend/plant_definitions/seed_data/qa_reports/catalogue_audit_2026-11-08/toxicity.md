## Scope matters

The table includes direct contradictions, dose/route qualifications and explicitly provisional group/contact findings. These are separate codes in the JSON, not equivalent levels of evidence. The distinction is not a binary dangerous/safe switch. Parsley and mint have dose-dependent pet concerns despite ordinary culinary use by people. Some edible plants can irritate sensitive skin. Lilium and Hemerocallis warnings must retain their cat-specific severity rather than implying identical effects in dogs. Podocarpus and Taxus are not interchangeable. NC State severity categories and pet-specific clinical severity are not one universal scale.

Leucojum vernum is the highest-priority confirmed harmless-label contradiction. Its matching source reports high-severity poisoning; this is not a cosmetic wording issue. Other warnings are milder or contact-specific and should remain qualified.

## Language and schema issues

Every seed toxicity trait is missing Indonesian, Russian and Turkish. The audit also checks clear positive/negative translation mismatches and identifies concrete malformed Polish/Hindi phrases. Native editorial validation of every localized phrase remains a separate task.

The schema should separate humans, cats/dogs and livestock, routes, parts and evidence confidence. A single short string cannot accurately represent an edible fruit with poisonous seeds, sap contact irritation, or a pet-only hazard. Use an explicit unknown state rather than guessing from missing literature.

ASPCA also cautions that its list is not exhaustive and that ingestion of ordinary plant material can cause digestive upset. [ASPCA methodology](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants).

## Expanded ASPCA source and identity review

All 106 existing ASPCA source references were fetched (66 unique URLs). 67 references match species, explicit synonyms, parent-species or explicit genus scope. 34 refer to a different scientific name without an established synonym match; 5 are general articles/index pages rather than an explicit species classification. They were not silently treated as correct species evidence.

After using matching explicit entries across the catalogue, 90 definitions have explicit ASPCA pet classifications: 59 toxic and 31 non-toxic to stated animals. Species/synonym/parent evidence and genus-only evidence remain distinguished in the JSON.

A confirmed opposite-direction error is **Rosmarinus officinalis**, currently “Toxic to pets”. ASPCA explicitly classifies rosemary as non-toxic to cats, dogs and horses. Align this with the synonymous **Salvia rosmarinus** record; do not mistake concentrated essential-oil exposures for the classification of the live plant. [ASPCA rosemary](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/rosemary).

Examples of identity gaps include using the Aloe vera page for other Aloe species, Camellia japonica for C. sinensis, Phalaenopsis for Coelogyne, Dendrobium gracilicaule for D. nobile, and pages about different Echeveria, Asplenium or Cissus species. These are excluded source links, not automatic instructions to flip the associated toxicity label. “Pet upset risk” on Araucaria heterophylla is compatible with ASPCA non-toxicity because ordinary plant material can cause digestive upset.
